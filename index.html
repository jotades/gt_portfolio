    About

    I’ve always been drawn to technical subjects, but what interests me most is how the pieces of a project fit together, and how to lead them there. Each step of my studies added a piece I was missing: computer science taught me how to make things work, industrial design why people would want them, and design engineering how to make them buildable. The PhD closed the loop: with OmniTrack I had to do all three at once, and to manage the project that held them together.

    Today I’m an industrial designer and engineer with an MSc from the Politecnico di Milano and a PhD in Mechanics and Advanced Engineering Sciences at the University of Bologna, carried out at CNR-STIIMA. I work where design and engineering meet: I start from people and research, and I stay with a product until its parts are drawn, analysed and ready to be made.

    For my PhD I designed and built OmniTrack, a platform that locates people and objects indoors and turns their movements into light, sound and vibration, and tested it with blind and visually impaired adults together with Utrecht University.

      WashB

      My MSc thesis: a mouthpiece that cleans teeth with ultrasound, so a good result no longer depends on how skilfully, or how often, someone brushes.

      Context
      MSc thesis
      Year
      2020
      Role
      ruolo / team
      Tools
      software
      Focus
      Research, product design, materials, FEM

      1Research

      I started from how people clean their teeth today: toothbrushes, floss, the technologies behind them, materials and daily habits. A benchmark of brushing, flossing and emerging technologies, together with a survey of 86 cases, pointed to the same weak spots. Conventional methods take time, can be invasive or even harmful, depend on manual dexterity, and still need a hygienist to remove plaque.

      How can we clean teeth with a non-invasive method that works whatever the user’s habits and dexterity?

      The answer I explored was ultrasound, which can break up biofilm even in the hardest-to-reach areas of the mouth.

      The survey: 86 people

      Fourteen questions on brushing, flossing and the dental hygienist, about six minutes each.

        2Brief

        Four requirements guided every design decision.

        • AdaptableFits different dental arches.
        • No motionThe user doesn’t have to move anything.
        • UltrasoundSafe and effective at removing biofilm.
        • PortableClean your teeth wherever you are.

        3Concept and product

        WashB is a transparent mouthpiece that holds the teeth in a small volume of liquid, sealed against the gums. An ultrasonic board inside makes the liquid vibrate, and a soft squeezer at the front fills and empties the chamber through a slit valve. Between uses it stays in a compact charging case.

        4How it’s used

        The user fills the mouthpiece, puts it on and lets a 20-second cycle clean one arch, then flips it for the other. A companion app shows the cleaning in real time, tracks progress and connects the user with their dentist.

        Operating conditions

        Liquid temperature25 °C
        TeethFully or partially immersed
        Ultrasound1.6 MHz, low intensity (< 3 W/cm²)
        UseTwice a day, 10–20 s per arch

        5The app

        The companion app pairs with the charging case, follows each cleaning in real time and keeps a record the user can share with their dentist.

        The prototype here is rebuilt from the app’s Unity project: the 3D arches with sixteen zones each, which the user taps to mark where more care is needed; the Top and Down views; the 3D scan that replaces the default arches with the user’s own; the dentist; and a short history of the toothbrush.

        Visual language

        Deep navy and teal, the colours of the WashB logo, on a pale surface. A wave separates each screen’s header from its content, the icons are drawn with a single line, and a rounded teal bar at the bottom always leads home. Teal and red mark where the device should clean and where it should not.

          6Engineering

          Ultrasound board

          The cleaning comes from CMUTs (capacitive micromachined ultrasonic transducers, supplied by Philips) laid out along the arch. Each membrane vibrates at 1.6 MHz and passes that vibration on to the liquid around the teeth.

          Squeezer

          The part the user presses has to be soft, safe in the mouth and printable. I chose medical-grade silicone for its biocompatibility, stiffness, resistance to water, solvents and UV, cost and availability for 3D printing, then checked its deformation with FEM under 12.5 N, the most the lips could press when measured with a force sensor and an Arduino. A first, flatter shape needed walls over 4 mm to stay within the 10 mm limit, so the chamber was redrawn deeper.

          OmniTrack

          An extensible IoT platform that locates people and objects indoors with ultra-wideband radio, and turns their movements into light, sound and vibration in real time. Designed around children with developmental coordination disorder, it was first evaluated with blind and visually impaired adults learning routes, with Utrecht University.

          The anchors, routes (start, landmarks and end), landmark profiles and feedback rules are those of the user study; the per-anchor calibration, smoothing and solver are OmniTrack’s, run in the browser on simulated UWB ranges.

          1The system

          Every unit is a 60 × 40 × 45 mm, 63 g case, 3D-printed in TPU and PETG, with a clip for a belt, a wrist strap or an object. Inside, an ESP32-S3 with a Qorvo DW3000 UWB radio measures the distance to the anchors, a BNO055 IMU gives the orientation, and an RGBW LED, a speaker on an I²S amplifier and a vibration motor give the feedback. The eight anchors use the same board, fixed at the corners and midpoints of the walls.

          The units talk to a PC over Bluetooth Low Energy: each is a GATT server, and a bridge per unit turns the link into a virtual serial port for its own micro-ROS agent. In ROS 2, the multilaterator turns the calibrated ranges into positions, and the orchestrator, a closed-loop controller, turns them into feedback commands that travel back the same way. The dashboard runs the task; sessions go to MongoDB and are analysed in OmniAnalytics.

          Inside a unit

          Every unit, tag or anchor, is built around the same module. Pins as set in the tag firmware.

          The Bluetooth link

          The units talk to the PC over Bluetooth Low Energy, chosen for its low power and simple implementation on small wearables, and built into the ESP32-S3 of every unit. Each tag is a GATT server: it advertises its name and exposes one custom service with two characteristics.

          The first carries micro-ROS itself. Through a custom transport in framed mode, the tag sends its ranging and inertial data as notifications, and reads the feedback commands that the PC writes to the same characteristic. The second reports the battery level when the PC asks for it.

          On the PC, one program scans for the tags and runs a bridge per tag as the GATT client; each bridge exposes its link as a virtual serial port for a micro-ROS agent of its own. A tag pings its agent until it answers, and after three missed pings it drops its publishers and waits again.

            3Calibration and accuracy
            
        
            Raw UWB ranges carry a distance-dependent bias, so every range is corrected before multilateration. The polynomial degree was never taken on fit alone: candidates from 1 to 5 were compared by cross-validation, leave-one-out for the 1D campaign and grouped by position for the 2D one, with AIC, AICc and BIC reported alongside.
            
        
            Where the criteria disagreed, the held-out error decided. One polynomial per anchor brought the mean horizontal error from 0.476 m to 0.184 m, and to 0.141 m on the study area: below the 0.2 m feedback step, so the signal does not flicker when the user stands still.
            
      
            
      
            
      
            
        
            
          
          
          
          
          
          
        
            
        
            
          
            
          
            
        
            
      
            

    
            

    
            
      
            
        
            4Distance into feedback
            
        
            In the study, the signal of a landmark started when the hand-held unit came within 1.0 m of it, grew in 0.2 m steps and reached its strongest step at 0.6 m. Each of the three landmark units had its own intensity range, so that the landmarks could be told apart; their order changed from trial to trial.
            
        
            The four conditions crossed modality and place: a pink-noise sound or a vibration from the unit in the hand, a sound from the unit on the landmark, or a floor patch found with the cane, with no signal from the platform.

            7Future work
            
        
            
          
          
        
            
        
            
        
            Smoothing that adapts to movement
            
        
            Every range goes through exponential smoothing, st = α xt + (1 − α) st−1. Today α is fixed at 0.4, a time constant of about 196 ms at 10 Hz: a compromise between a steady signal when the user stands still and a quick one when they move.
            
        
            The next step is to let α follow the movement: a regression with an MLP neural network that reads the IMU and the UWB ranges and predicts α at every sample.
            
        
            The network learns to recognise movement patterns on its own.
            
        
            When it detects a sudden acceleration, typical of a change of direction, it pushes α towards 0.9 so that the feedback no longer lags behind. When the user stands still it lowers α to 0.1, to hold back the millimetre jitter of UWB.

            Academic project, designed around services such as MiMoto and Ecooltra. Not commissioned by either company.
            
      
            
        
            Context
            MSc project, corso / team
            
        
            Year
            2019
            
        
            Role

            Size

            Focus
            Service design, product design, UX
            
      
            
    
            
    

    
            
      
            
        
            1Context
            
        
            For a sharing service, a scooter with a flat battery is a scooter nobody can rent. I framed the problem with a twist on Maslow’s pyramid, where battery and Wi-Fi sit underneath physiological needs, and benchmarked portable EV chargers, robotic lawnmowers, generators and wireless phone chargers.
            
      
            
      
    
            

    
            
      
            
        
            2Brief
            
        
            Six requirements, from where the robot can go to how it’s called.
            
      
            
      
              
        
            • AreaWorks within defined zones, such as limited-traffic and pedestrian areas.
              • 
        
            • BatteryCarries enough energy to charge several Askoll eS2 scooters in one trip.
              • 
        
            • CompatibilityThe charging system adapts to a wide range of small vehicles.
              • 
        
            • ChargingReaches the scooter’s socket easily, and the connection releases on its own.
              • 
        
            • StationReturns to a base station to recharge itself.
              • 
        
            • AppCalled from the sharing service’s own app.
              • 
      
            
    
            

    
            
      
            
        
            3Concept and product
            
        
            RED 6.3 has two main volumes. The body carries the service battery; the head houses a linear actuator that pushes a square charging pad against the scooter. Four proximity sensors, headlights and signal lights let it move safely among people.
            
      
            
      
            
        
        
      
            
    
            

    
            
      
            
        
            4App
            
        
            Riders follow the robot from the sharing app: notifications from RED 6.3, the scooters on the map with their charge, and the details of the one they pick.
            
        
            The prototype is rebuilt from the app’s screens in the portfolio, as its original files are not available. The map, plates and addresses are sample data.
            
        
            Visual language
            
        
            The app takes on the sharing service’s yellow for its bars and puts everything else on a dark ground, so the map and the scooters stand out outdoors. Teal means charge and action, red a battery running low; the icons are white and drawn with a single line.

              D.
              
      
              A steel spring baseplate for skateboard trucks that makes the truck lighter and better at absorbing shocks. I used finite element analysis to find a safe thickness and to compare three shapes for different ways of riding.
              
      
              
        
              Context
              corso
              
        
              Year
              2018
              
        
              Role
              ruolo
              
        
              Software
              es. Abaqus, SolidWorks
              
        
              Focus
              FEM, material selection, design optimisation
              
      
              
    
              
    

    
              
      
              
        
              1The part
              
        
              The spring baseplate replaces the rigid baseplate of a standard truck. The rest of the assembly stays familiar: kingpin, bushings, pivot bushing and hanger.
              
      
              
      
    
              

    
              
      
              
        
              2Material and mesh
              
        
              Material selection pointed to stainless steel (Young’s modulus 189–210 GPa). I modelled the plate as a thin shell with STRI65 elements: 6-node quadratic triangles with five degrees of freedom per node, three translations and two in-plane rotations.
              
      
              
      
    
              

    
              
      
              
        
              3Thickness
              
        
              Two load cases: normal riding, 500 N on one truck, and an impact of 2,000 N, each split between two points on the seat of the hanger. The plate is constrained where it’s screwed to the deck. With a yield strength of 1,000 MPa and a 1.5 safety factor, the allowable stress is 667 MPa.
              
        
              Result: 3 mm is enough for normal riding, 5 mm is needed to withstand impacts.

              5Shape optimisation
              
        
              For an elastic part, stiffness is the ratio between force and displacement, K = F/Δx. I compared three geometries of the plate, each suited to a different way of riding.

              RehaBalloon
              
      
              A balloon that coaches hand rehabilitation. A pressure sensor inside reads every squeeze: a light ring shows when you’re holding the right pressure, and a small neural network on the board counts your repetitions.
              
      
              Built in about 24 hours at the Hacking with Hardware workshop of the Media Informatics group, LMU Munich, with Jason Hoffman and Pallabi cognome.
              
      
              
        
              Context
              24-hour hackathon, LMU Munich
              
        
              Year
              2023
              
        
              Role
              il tuo ruolo
              
        
              Hardware
              XIAO nRF52840 Sense, BMP280, LED ring
              
        
              Focus
              Interaction design, physical computing, TinyML

              Press and hold the balloon to squeeze it, release to let go. On a keyboard, hold the space bar.

              1Idea
              
        
              Hand rehabilitation means repeating the same exercises at home, often without knowing whether you’re doing them right. A balloon is cheap, soft and familiar, so we turned it into the device: put the electronics inside, inflate it, and it can measure every squeeze.
              
        
              The prototype supports two kinds of exercise. Holding a steady pressure trains fine motor control; repeated squeezes, with the hands or pressing the arms together, train strength and are counted for each session.

              2How it works
              
        
              A Seeed XIAO nRF52840 Sense with a BMP280 pressure sensor sits inside the inflated balloon. When it starts, it records the resting pressure as a baseline, then compares every reading with it. The difference drives the light ring and, over Bluetooth LE, can be sent to a phone.
              
      
              
      
              
    
              

    
              
      
              
        
              Machine learning on the device
              
        
              3Teaching it to count
              
        
              To tell a normal squeeze from a quick one, we recorded our own: each time the pressure rose 7.5 hPa above the baseline, the board captured the next 119 samples. With 79 normal and 35 quick squeezes we trained a TensorFlow Lite model small enough to run on the microcontroller.
              
        
              The recordings show the difference the model learns: normal squeezes keep the pressure up for the whole window, quick ones let go halfway.

Before it could help patients it needs research with physiotherapists and patients, a proper enclosure for the electronics and testing across real rehabilitation sessions. The concept below is a first answer to the enclosure.

              5A reusable core
              
        
              In the prototype the board simply sat loose inside the balloon. The concept moves all the electronics into a core that plugs into the balloon’s neck. The balloon becomes a cheap, replaceable cover, so each patient can have their own, while the core is charged and reused.
              
        
              Inside the balloon, a diffuser dome carries the LED ring so the whole balloon glows, and a small port lets the sensor read the air pressure. Outside, the body holds the board and the battery, with USB-C for charging. An inflation valve matters more than it looks: the light thresholds are measured from the starting pressure, so pumping the balloon to the same starting point each time makes exercises repeatable.

              2A road made of blocks

              Pick a block and change it, or add new ones: each block joins the road as a new tile, and as the line the program prints for it. The program’s own two example blocks break some of these rules, so the road starts with one carriageway.

                3Why Logitech’s SDK
                
        
                A direct-drive base turns the motor’s shaft straight into the steering wheel, so it can push back with real force. But Simucube had no software of its own to talk to Unity, and without it none of the simulator’s scenes could have used force feedback.

                4Calibration

To know the newton-metres at the rim, a wire was run tangent to the wheel, 17 cm from its centre, and loaded with known weights: a 0.634 kg hanger, then one kilogram more at a time. Because the wire leaves the wheel at a tangent, the torque is simply weight × g × 0.17 m. For every load, with the spring at 85% saturation and 75% coefficient, the SDK’s axis value X was recorded.

                The die is modelled and baked in Blender with Cycles; the meshes and textures here are exactly what came out of it. The white outlines on the texture are the low poly’s UV islands.
                
    
                


    
                
      
                
        
                1Why bake
                
        
                A game engine draws a model faster when it has fewer materials and fewer polygons. Baking computes in advance what a detailed model looks like and stores it in textures, so a much simpler model can look the same.

                2One atlas instead of three materials
                
        
                The high poly uses three materials: the body, the pips and the red pip of the one. The low poly gets a UV map that gives each face its own square, and Cycles bakes the colour of the high poly into that image. A single material on the low poly reads it.
                
      
                
      
    
                

    
                
      
                
        
                3Detail into a normal map
                
        
                The rounded edges and the 21 dimples exist only on the high poly. Baked as a tangent-space normal map, they make the simple cube catch the light as if it were rounded and dimpled: flat blue means no change, the gradients along the borders are the rounded edges.
                
        
                A normal map changes the light, not the outline. That is why the low poly keeps a small chamfer on its edges: 26 faces instead of 6, enough for the silhouette not to look razor sharp, while the normal map does the actual rounding.
                
      
                
      
    
                

    
                
      
                
        
                4The cage
                
        
                The low poly has only a small chamfer while the high poly is fully rounded, so rays cast from the low poly’s surface would meet the high poly at the wrong point near the corners. The cage, the low poly scaled by 1.08, wraps the whole high poly and gives Cycles a surface to cast the rays from.
